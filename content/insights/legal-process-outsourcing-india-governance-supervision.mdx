Legal process outsourcing in India is no longer judged on price. It is judged on whether the work can be relied upon — and under Indian law, the instructing firm's responsibility for that work does not transfer with the files. This note sets out the framework that determines whether distributed legal work is dependable: what Indian law permits an offshore legal team to do, where professional responsibility sits, how scope is defined, how output is verified, and how confidential and privileged material is handled. It is written for law firms and in-house legal departments evaluating offshore legal services in India, and for those already engaged who want a framework against which to test the arrangement.

## The question is not where the work is performed

Legal work increasingly arrives in volume. Contract populations, transaction records, disclosure sets, research tasks and compliance obligations consume capacity that cannot be scaled by hiring alone, and will not wait for the quarter in which hiring becomes possible.

The commercial case for distributing defined parts of that work is well understood: additional capacity, structured document handling, and a cost base that permits a volume of review that would otherwise be uneconomic. That case is not in dispute, and it is not what this note is about.

The question that determines whether such an arrangement succeeds is different. It is whether work can be delegated without compromising professional judgment, document integrity, confidentiality or accountability — and, critically, whether the instructing lawyer's own exposure has been managed rather than merely relocated.

It has not been relocated. Two features of Indian law make that concrete, and both are frequently absent from the discussion.

## Legal process outsourcing in India: what the law actually permits

The governing authority is *Bar Council of India v. A.K. Balaji*, decided by the Supreme Court of India on 13 March 2018. The Court held that foreign law firms and foreign lawyers cannot practise the profession of law in India, in litigation or in non-litigation work, unless they satisfy the Advocates Act, 1961 and the Bar Council of India Rules. Practice, the Court held, is not confined to appearance in court: it includes giving opinions, drafting instruments and participating in conferences involving legal discussion.

For legal outsourcing services in India, the operative part of the judgment is what the Court said about business process outsourcing. The Madras High Court had held that companies providing a range of customised and integrated services — word processing, secretarial support, transcription, proof-reading, travel desk support and similar services — fell outside the Advocates Act, 1961 and the Bar Council of India Rules. The Supreme Court modified that direction, and the modification is the point.

The label a provider attaches to its services is not conclusive. The Court will look at the pith and substance of the work. If, in substance, the services amount to the practice of law, the Advocates Act, 1961 applies, and each case falls to be decided on its own facts.

Two consequences follow, and they are structural rather than cosmetic.

**First, the boundary is drawn by the content of the work, not by the contract.** An engagement letter describing an assignment as "research support" does not make it research support. If the deliverable is, in substance, legal advice on the law of a foreign jurisdiction, the characterisation in the paperwork will not survive examination.

**Second, the boundary is therefore a drafting problem that must be solved before the work begins.** This is why scope definition is not administrative hygiene. It is the mechanism by which a lawful and defensible division of labour is created — the instructing lawyer retaining advice and judgment, the offshore team performing defined, reviewable work within instructions.

An offshore law firm support arrangement in India that respects that boundary is on firm ground. One that blurs it is exposed, and the exposure runs in both directions.

## Why the instructing firm's exposure survives delegation

The second feature concerns data, and it produces a result that surprises most firms when it is first explained to them.

Under **Section 17(1)(d) of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023**, the provisions of Chapter II — except sub-sections (1) and (5) of Section 8 — together with Chapter III and Section 16, do not apply where personal data of data principals not within the territory of India is processed pursuant to a contract entered into with a person outside India by a person based in India.

That clause describes the typical offshore legal services engagement in India precisely. A United States or United Kingdom firm instructs an India-based team to process documents concerning individuals outside India. The consent architecture, the notice obligations and the restrictions on cross-border transfer are switched off.

It would be a serious error to read that as a relaxation.

Two provisions survive the exemption, and they are the two that matter. **Section 8(1) of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** provides that a data fiduciary is responsible for complying with the Act and the rules made under it in respect of any processing undertaken by it *or on its behalf by a data processor* — and that this responsibility applies irrespective of any agreement to the contrary. **Section 8(5)** requires the data fiduciary to protect personal data in its possession or under its control, including in respect of processing undertaken on its behalf by a data processor, by taking reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach.

Read together with Section 17(1)(d), the position is this. For cross-border legal support work, the statute largely withdraws its own procedural machinery — and retains the allocation of responsibility. Accountability cannot be contracted away. Security obligations attach to the instructing party in respect of work performed by someone else, in another country, on its behalf.

The practical consequence is the thesis of this note. Where the statute does not supply the governance, the engagement does. The contract, the workflow, the access controls and the review structure are not supplementary protections layered on top of a statutory regime. In this category of work they are substantially the whole of it.

A firm that treats offshore governance as procurement diligence has misread where its own risk sits.

## Define the assignment before allocating responsibility

An instruction to "review the contracts" or "assist with due diligence" appears straightforward and is not.

Does the reviewer extract specified clauses, identify deviations from an agreed template, flag unusual provisions, reconcile dates and parties, or prepare a substantive risk analysis? What constitutes a completed deliverable? Which questions require escalation rather than independent resolution?

A properly defined assignment identifies the documents in scope, the tasks to be completed, the required output, the deadline, the review process and the circumstances in which clarification is mandatory.

For contract abstraction, the output specification might require governing law, term, renewal mechanics, termination rights, liability limitations and notice requirements, each supported by a reference to the provision relied upon. For transaction due diligence, the process might require a document index, a record of missing materials, identification of inconsistencies between documents, and an exception register for matters requiring further investigation.

The object is not to reduce legal work to a mechanical exercise. It is to define the assignment with sufficient precision that the work can be performed consistently, that its results can be independently checked, and — returning to *A.K. Balaji* — that what has been delegated is demonstrably not the practice of law.

Precision at this stage is also where the economics are won. Rework is the principal hidden cost of offshore legal support, and rework is almost always traceable to an instruction that was never specified rather than to a reviewer who was careless.

## Separate execution from legal judgment, and record who holds each

Delegating a defined task does not remove the need for professional oversight. It relocates the point at which oversight must be exercised.

A legal support workflow should distinguish, explicitly, between four categories of activity — collecting and organising information; identifying departures from agreed criteria; conducting directed research; and preparing draft materials — and the separate category of making substantive legal decisions.

The distinction sharpens where a matter touches more than one jurisdiction. Research or drafting support concerning United States securities matters must operate within the instructions and review structure established by appropriately qualified United States counsel. The same applies to work governed by the law of the United Kingdom, Canada, Singapore or any other jurisdiction. The offshore team is working to instructions; it is not advising on foreign law, and the arrangement should make that impossible to confuse.

An effective structure identifies four named roles: the person assigning the work, the professional responsible for supervision, the reviewer authorised to approve the deliverable, and the individual who decides substantive legal questions.

Naming them does more than allocate tasks. It prevents assumptions from being mistaken for conclusions, eliminates duplicated review, and ensures that an unresolved question reaches a decision-maker rather than being resolved by whoever encountered it first.

## Build quality control into the process, and make it evidentiary

Quality should not depend on the experience or attentiveness of the individual performing the assignment. It should be produced by a repeatable process.

For document-intensive work, the controls that earn their place are standardised review templates, defined completion criteria, source-document references, exception logs and a final verification stage.

The test of such a process is specific. A reviewer should be able to establish, without reconstructing the work, where an extracted fact came from, whether the underlying document supports it, whether a discrepancy was identified, and whether a question requiring legal judgment was escalated.

That traceability is usually described as a quality measure. It is better understood as an evidentiary one. Work produced offshore may have to be defended — in a regulatory enquiry, in a professional negligence claim, in a dispute about what diligence was performed before a transaction closed, or in cross-examination about the completeness of a disclosure exercise. A process that cannot show its own workings will not assist in any of those settings.

An unrecorded renewal provision, an inconsistent company name or an undocumented missing document is a small failure at the point of occurrence and a significant one at the point of reliance.

Assurance should be proportionate. Document indexing and research informing a substantive submission do not warrant the same level of review. The principle does not vary: the completed work should be verifiable, exceptions should be visible, and the reviewer should not have to rebuild the process to establish what was done.

## Treat confidentiality, privilege and security as workflow requirements

Legal documents contain commercially sensitive information, privileged communications, personal data and material subject to contractual or regulatory restriction. A confidentiality undertaking is necessary and is not sufficient.

Three distinct protections are in play, and they are frequently conflated.

**Privilege.** In India, professional communications are protected under **Section 132 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023**, which succeeded Section 126 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 and has applied since 1 July 2024. Privilege under the law of the instructing jurisdiction is a separate question governed by that jurisdiction's rules, and whether it survives disclosure to a support provider depends on how the engagement is structured. It should be addressed in the engagement terms, not assumed.

**Data protection.** As set out above, Section 8(1) and Section 8(5) of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 survive the Section 17(1)(d) exemption. Security is an obligation of the instructing party discharged through the offshore workflow.

**Contractual and regulatory restriction.** Many documents carry their own confidentiality regime, imposed by the agreement under which they were produced or by the regulator of the sector in which the client operates. These bind independently of the two above.

Before documents move, the parties should settle the applicable authorisations, confidentiality obligations, conflict-checking requirements, approved transfer methods, access permissions, retention expectations and the procedure for a suspected incident.

Access should be limited to those who require it for the assignment. Instructions should state whether documents may be downloaded, copied, transferred to third-party platforms or retained after completion.

Where automated or AI-assisted tools are used, their permitted role and the handling of uploaded material should be expressly agreed. Confidential or privileged material should not enter an unapproved system because doing so would accelerate a task. The acceleration is real and the exposure is permanent.

These questions are cheap to resolve before the document flow begins and expensive to resolve afterwards.

## Measure delivery against agreed criteria

A reliable arrangement requires a shared definition of successful delivery.

Turnaround time is relevant and insufficient. Fast delivery that generates substantial rework, or that leaves material exceptions unidentified, has negative value: it consumes the instructing team's time twice and conceals the fact until late.

Depending on the assignment, the measures worth tracking are completion within the agreed timeframe, accuracy against source documents, consistency of categorisation, the number and nature of corrections required, completeness of exception reporting, and compliance with document-handling instructions.

These are read in context. A complex research task is not assessed as extraction of standardised contract data is assessed.

Equally important is the escalation mechanism. If a document is missing, an instruction is ambiguous, or an unexpected legal issue surfaces, the team should know when and how to raise it — and should be confident that raising it is the expected behaviour.

An effective process makes uncertainty visible early. A defective one rewards a team for guessing in order to meet a deadline, and the cost of that guess is discovered by the instructing lawyer at the worst available moment.

## Begin with a defined scope, then refine the model

Before expanding an offshore arrangement, establish that the workflow functions as designed.

A bounded initial assignment will reveal whether the instructions are sufficiently precise, whether the output meets the required standard, whether the review structure is proportionate to the risk, and whether the agreed timelines are realistic. The parties can then examine what worked, where clarification was required, and what should change.

This is more informative than any capability statement, including this one. It permits the operating model to be assessed against actual deliverables and agreed criteria, without assuming that competence in one category of work establishes suitability for every other.

Expansion should follow demonstrated performance, with scope and supervision adjusted to the complexity and risk of each new category of assignment.

## How Sagar & Sagar structures offshore law firm support in India

Sagar & Sagar Law Offices has been in continuous legal practice in New Delhi since 2000, with a litigation and transactional practice in banking and finance, debt recovery, insolvency, corporate and commercial law, regulatory compliance and dispute resolution. The firm's [legal process outsourcing practice](/lpo/) is staffed and supervised from within that practice rather than operated as a separate processing business.

That structure is the firm's answer to the governance question this note describes, and it has three consequences.

**Work is produced by practising lawyers.** Research, drafting and review for overseas clients is performed by qualified Indian lawyers who conduct contentious and advisory matters in their own right. They understand how the material will be used, and how it will be tested, in a live matter — because they have had their own work tested. A chronology prepared by someone who has conducted a cross-examination is a different document from one prepared by someone who has not.

**Supervision is held by a partner, not by a process.** The firm's offshore legal services work is supervised by **Adv. Rajeev Sagar**, the firm's Founding and Managing Partner, who [leads the firm's litigation and dispute resolution practice](/about/rajeev-sagar/) and appears before the Supreme Court of India, the High Courts and the principal tribunals. The supervisory structure described above — the four named roles, the escalation route, the final verification — terminates in a partner who practises in the subject matter, rather than in a quality-assurance function reporting on metrics. For delicate matters, where the question is whether something should be escalated at all, that is the distinction that determines the outcome.

**The scope is deliberately wide.** The firm's domestic practice covers [banking, finance and debt recovery](/practice-areas/banking-finance-debt-recovery/), [insolvency](/practice-areas/insolvency-bankruptcy-ibc/), [white collar and financial crime defence](/practice-areas/white-collar-financial-crime-defence/), [corporate and commercial matters](/practice-areas/corporate-ma-private-equity-capital-markets/), [arbitration and dispute resolution](/practice-areas/arbitration-mediation-adr/), [regulatory compliance](/practice-areas/regulatory-competition-compliance/) and [intellectual property](/practice-areas/intellectual-property/). Offshore support assignments frequently begin in one of those areas and develop into another; a document review that surfaces a security interest becomes a recovery question, and a diligence exercise that surfaces a related-party transfer becomes an insolvency question. Where the supporting team practises across those boundaries, the development is recognised rather than missed.

The firm is institutionally empanelled with several of India's public sector and private sector banks, housing finance companies, non-banking financial companies and financial institutions. Empanelment follows a structured evaluation by an institution's law department of qualifications, experience, court coverage and professional conduct, and is distinct from ordinary client engagement. For an overseas client it is a useful proxy: the firm's work is already subject to recurring scrutiny by institutional legal departments. One of the firm's founding partners, **Sanjeev Sagar, was designated a Senior Advocate by the High Court of Delhi in November 2024** — a distinction conferred by the Court in recognition of ability and standing at the Bar.

**On cost.** The commercial advantage of alternative legal service providers in India is usually explained by reference to the cost base, which is real but incomplete as an account. Two further factors determine whether an engagement is economically sensible over its life. The first is rework, which is the dominant hidden cost of offshore work and is controlled by scope definition rather than by rate. The second is the cost of the review layer: where supervision sits inside the practice performing the work, it is part of the engagement rather than a separate quantum of senior time purchased to check someone else's output. An arrangement that requires the instructing firm to rebuild its own review function around the deliverable has not reduced cost. It has moved it.

In every engagement model, professional responsibility for the matter, the client relationship, and any appearance or filing in the foreign jurisdiction remains entirely with the instructing attorney or in-house team. The firm's role is defined, supervised and documented at the outset.
