A founders' agreement records the terms between the people starting a business — equity split, roles, vesting, intellectual property and what happens if one of them leaves. A shareholders' agreement governs the relationship between a company's shareholders once outside investors come in, dealing with board composition, reserved matters, transfer restrictions, exit and information rights. In India, the critical point is that neither instrument binds the company by itself: the company is bound by its articles of association, and under <span className="statute-ref">Section 10 of the Companies Act, 2013</span> it is the memorandum and articles that bind the company and its members. A right agreed in a shareholders' agreement but never written into the articles may be enforceable between the signatories as a contract and still be unenforceable against the company.

## 1. Why founders sign the wrong document first

Most Indian startups begin with no written agreement at all. The founders know each other, the equity split is understood, and documentation feels like a cost that can wait until the first term sheet.

It can wait. The problem is what it costs when it stops waiting.

By the time a seed investor conducts diligence, the questions are specific. Who owns the code written before incorporation? Is the equity split documented? Is there vesting, and has any of it vested? Did the founder who left eighteen months ago sign anything? A gap in any of these is not fatal, but it is resolved at the worst possible moment — under time pressure, with the investor holding the leverage, and frequently at the cost of the remaining founders' equity.

The second, quieter problem is that founders who do sign something often sign a template that is a shareholders' agreement in form and a founders' agreement in name. The two instruments do different work, and the difference matters.

## 2. What each instrument is actually for

**A founders' agreement governs the relationship between the people building the business.** It is signed early, often before or around incorporation, and frequently when the company has no outside shareholders at all. Its concerns are equity, contribution, roles, time commitment, intellectual property and departure.

**A shareholders' agreement governs the relationship between the company's owners.** It arrives with the first institutional round. Its concerns are governance, control, protection of the investor's economic position, and the mechanics of exit.

A founders' agreement does not stop being relevant when a shareholders' agreement is signed — but if the two say different things about vesting or departure, the later document and the articles will usually prevail. Founders who negotiated a careful vesting schedule at inception and then signed a standard investment document without checking the interaction sometimes discover the original terms have been displaced.

## 3. The enforceability problem every founder should understand

This is the point at which Indian practice diverges from the templates circulating online, and it is worth stating precisely.

Under **<span className="statute-ref">Section 10 of the Companies Act, 2013</span>**, the memorandum and articles, when registered, bind the company and its members. A shareholders' agreement is a contract between its signatories. The question of what happens when the agreement says one thing and the articles are silent has a long and unsettled history.

In ***V.B. Rangaraj v. V.B. Gopalakrishnan***, the Supreme Court held that a restriction on transfer of shares contained in a shareholders' agreement but not reflected in the articles was binding on neither the company nor the shareholders. For years that was the governing position.

In ***Vodafone International Holdings BV v. Union of India***, the Supreme Court recorded that it did not subscribe to that view, observing that shareholders may enter into any agreement in the best interest of the company provided its provisions do not run contrary to the articles. It did not, however, expressly overrule *Rangaraj*, and High Courts have since gone different ways — the Bombay High Court giving effect to inter se arrangements not reflected in the articles, the Delhi High Court declining to enforce them.

The **<span className="statute-ref">proviso to Section 58(2) of the Companies Act, 2013</span>** states that a contract or arrangement in respect of transfer of securities is enforceable as a contract. That provision sits within the section dealing with **public** companies. For private companies — which is what almost every startup is — the position is not settled by statute in the same terms.

**The practical consequence is simple, and it is the single most useful thing in this article.** Negotiating a right is only half the work. If the right matters — a transfer restriction, a reserved matter, a board seat, a pre-emption right — it should be reflected in the articles of association, by special resolution, at the same time as the agreement is signed. Amending the articles is procedurally straightforward. Discovering, three years later, that a carefully drafted clause cannot be enforced against the company is not.

## 4. Clauses in a founders' agreement that should never be left out

**Equity split, with the basis recorded.** Not just the percentages, but what they reflect — capital contributed, time committed, IP brought in, roles. When a split is questioned later, the recorded rationale is what makes it defensible.

**Founder vesting.** The provision founders most resist and most need. Equity vesting over time, typically with a cliff, means a founder who leaves in month eight does not retain a quarter of the company. There is no statutory requirement for founder vesting in India; it is purely contractual, which makes the drafting load-bearing. Investors will ask for it in any event — agreeing it between founders first means agreeing it while everyone is still aligned.

**Good leaver and bad leaver.** What happens to unvested — and sometimes vested — equity on departure, distinguishing resignation, removal for cause, death and incapacity. Define "cause" precisely; a vague definition becomes the dispute.

**Intellectual property assignment.** Everything created for the business, including work done before incorporation, assigned to the company in writing. This is a standard diligence item and a common gap, particularly where a technical founder built an early version personally.

**Roles, decision-making and deadlock.** Who decides what, what requires unanimity, and what happens when two equal founders disagree. Fifty-fifty splits with no deadlock mechanism are a recurring source of paralysis.

**Confidentiality and restrictive covenants.** Note the limit: **<span className="statute-ref">Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872</span>** renders agreements in restraint of trade void, subject to the statutory exception, and Indian courts have generally declined to enforce post-employment non-compete restrictions. Confidentiality and non-solicitation obligations stand on different footing. A founders' agreement that relies on a broad post-exit non-compete is relying on something that may not hold.

**Founder compensation and expense.** Modest, but disputes about who was paid what, and who funded what personally, are common and entirely avoidable.

## 5. Clauses in a shareholders' agreement that decide control

**Board composition and observer rights.** How many directors each party appoints, quorum requirements, and whether a meeting can proceed without the investor director present. Quorum is where control quietly sits.

**Reserved matters.** The list of decisions requiring investor consent — issuing shares, incurring debt beyond a threshold, changing the business, related-party transactions, selling the company. This list is the real governance document. A long list can make ordinary operations require consent; a short one may leave an investor without protection. It should be negotiated line by line, not accepted as boilerplate.

**Transfer restrictions.** Right of first refusal, right of first offer, tag-along and drag-along. **Drag-along deserves particular attention from founders**: it permits a defined majority to compel minority shareholders to sell. Founders who become minority holders after successive rounds can find themselves dragged into a sale at a price they would not have chosen. The thresholds and carve-outs are worth real negotiation.

**Anti-dilution.** Protection for the investor if a later round prices lower. Full ratchet and broad-based weighted average produce materially different outcomes for founder equity in a down round. The distinction is not technical detail; it is the difference between dilution and significant dilution.

**Liquidation preference.** Who is paid first on a sale, and whether the preference is participating. In a modest exit, preference terms can determine whether founders receive anything at all.

**Information and inspection rights.** Reporting obligations. Unremarkable until they become a compliance burden on a small team.

**Founder lock-in and exit obligations.** Investors typically require founders to remain for a defined period and restrict founder share sales. These interact directly with the founders' agreement — and are where inconsistency between the two documents most often surfaces.

**Dispute resolution.** Arbitration clause, seat, governing law, number of arbitrators. Drafted carelessly, this clause produces a dispute of its own before the substantive dispute is reached.

## 6. The ESOP point founders routinely miss

Startups create an option pool early, and founders often assume they can participate in it.

Under **<span className="statute-ref">Section 62(1)(b) of the Companies Act, 2013</span>** read with the relevant rule in the <span className="statute-ref">Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014</span>, options may not be granted to a promoter, a person belonging to the promoter group, or a director holding more than ten per cent of the outstanding equity directly or indirectly.

There is an exception that matters commercially: a startup recognised by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade is exempt from that exclusion for **ten years from the date of incorporation**. A founder in a DPIIT-recognised startup who holds a significant stake and is classified as a promoter may therefore receive options within that window, subject to shareholder approval and the other conditions.

Two practical implications follow. DPIIT recognition is not merely a branding exercise. And for a founder diluted across successive rounds, a grant made during that window has a defined shelf life — the exemption runs from incorporation, not from when anyone thinks about it.

## 7. The moment both documents are tested

Diligence is where drafting is examined, and the pattern is consistent. Investors look for a documented equity split, executed IP assignments covering every contributor, vesting with the position accurately recorded, clean cap table reconciliation, properly passed board and shareholder resolutions, and signed documentation from departed founders.

Where a gap exists, the transaction does not usually collapse. It is repriced, or an indemnity is inserted, or completion is delayed while a founder who left two years ago is located and asked to sign. Each of those outcomes is paid for by the founders.
