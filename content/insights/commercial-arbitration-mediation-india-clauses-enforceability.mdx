<p className="text-xs uppercase tracking-wide text-accent">Executive summary</p>

A dispute resolution clause is not boilerplate. It is the only part of a commercial contract that operates after the relationship has broken down, and the choices made in it — seat, institution, number of arbitrators, interim relief, pre-litigation steps — determine how long a dispute takes, what it costs, and whether the eventual award can be executed. Indian arbitration law has moved substantially since 2023: a seven-Judge Bench has settled the position on unstamped arbitration agreements, a Constitution Bench has confirmed a limited judicial power to modify awards, and the Supreme Court has restated how the seat of arbitration is identified. The Mediation Act, 2023 adds a further layer, though its operative provisions and its commencement position need to be checked before any clause relies on them.

**Key takeaways**

- Under **Section 36 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**, an award is enforced as if it were a decree of a civil court, and a challenge under **Section 34** no longer stays enforcement automatically. The award-debtor must apply separately for a stay.
- Under **Section 29A of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**, a domestic award must be made within twelve months of completion of pleadings, extendable by six months with party consent and thereafter only by a court.
- For a commercial suit of Specified Value, **Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015** requires pre-institution mediation unless the suit contemplates urgent interim relief. The Supreme Court has held this requirement mandatory.
- A venue named in the clause will usually be treated as the seat unless there are significant contrary indications — which makes a loosely drafted clause a jurisdictional risk, not merely an untidy one.

## Why the dispute resolution clause is negotiated last and matters most

In most commercial negotiations the dispute resolution clause is settled in the final hour, often by reference to a precedent nobody has re-read. The commercial team has moved on to closing. The clause is treated as machinery.

The difficulty is that every other clause in the contract assumes performance. The dispute resolution clause assumes failure. It is the one provision drafted for a state of affairs in which the counterparty is no longer cooperating, may be dissipating assets, and has every incentive to delay. By the time it is read in earnest, it cannot be amended except by agreement with the party it is being used against.

Litigation before the civil courts remains available and, for some disputes — shareholder and oppression claims among them, as noted in our piece on [founders' and shareholders' agreements](/insights/founders-agreement-vs-shareholders-agreement-india/) — remains correct. But for commercial contracts of any size, the practical question is rarely "arbitration or court". It is a series of narrower questions: what the parties must do before either, who decides, where, under whose rules, and what happens on the day the award is handed down. Those questions are answered in the clause or they are answered later, expensively, by a court.

## Arbitration, mediation and litigation compared

The three routes are not substitutes. They sit at different points on a scale running from party control to judicial determination, and a well-constructed contract frequently uses more than one.

## The framework, as it currently stands

### Arbitration

The governing statute is the **Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**, amended in 2015, 2019 and 2021. Its architecture is familiar: **Section 7** defines the arbitration agreement; **Section 8** requires a judicial authority to refer parties to arbitration; **Section 11** governs appointment; **Section 16** gives the tribunal competence to rule on its own jurisdiction; **Sections 9 and 17** provide for interim measures by the court and the tribunal respectively; **Section 34** sets out the grounds for setting aside; and **Sections 36 and 37** govern enforcement and appeal.

Four developments since 2023 have changed how that architecture operates in practice.

### Stamping is no longer a gateway defence

In *In Re: Interplay between Arbitration Agreements under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 and the Indian Stamp Act, 1899* (2023 INSC 1066), a seven-Judge Bench held that an arbitration agreement contained in an unstamped or insufficiently stamped instrument is not void. Non-stamping is a curable defect: **Section 35 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899** bars admissibility until duty and penalty are paid, but does not render the instrument a nullity. At the referral stage the court examines only whether an arbitration agreement prima facie exists; questions of stamping are for the tribunal. The earlier decision in *N.N. Global* was overruled. For an award-creditor, this closes a line of attack that had been used to stall references for years.

### Non-signatories can be brought in

In *Cox and Kings Ltd. v. SAP India Pvt. Ltd.* (2023 INSC 1051), a Constitution Bench confirmed that the group of companies doctrine has a place in Indian arbitration law, so that a non-signatory within a corporate group may in defined circumstances be bound by an arbitration agreement. In group structures where the contracting entity is thinly capitalised, this matters at the enforcement stage as much as at the reference stage.

### Awards can be modified, but only within narrow limits

In *Gayatri Balasamy v. M/s ISG Novasoft Technologies Ltd.* (2025 INSC 605), a five-Judge Bench held by majority that a court exercising jurisdiction under **Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996** has a limited power to modify an award: where an invalid portion is severable from the rest; where there is a manifest clerical, computational or typographical error; in relation to post-award interest in defined circumstances; and, in the case of the Supreme Court, under **Article 142 of the Constitution of India**. The decision resolves a long-running divergence, but it is a narrow power and it is not a merits appeal.

### Seat is determined by the clause, not by inference from the contract

In *M/s Arif Azim Co. Ltd. v. M/s Micromax Informatics FZE* (2024 INSC 850), the Supreme Court applied the *Shashoua* principle: where a clause designates a place as the venue of arbitration and there are no significant contrary indications, that place is the seat. On the facts, Dubai was held to be the seat, with the consequence that the courts of Dubai had supervisory jurisdiction and the Indian courts could not appoint an arbitrator under **Section 11 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**. A clause that names a city casually therefore selects a supervisory court.

Two further points of practical weight. First, since the 2015 amendment, an application under **Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996** does not operate as an automatic stay of enforcement; the award-debtor must apply under **Section 36(2)** of that Act, and under **Section 36(3)** the court applies the principles governing stay of a money decree, which in practice usually means a deposit or security. The attempt to reverse this by inserting **Section 87** into the same Act in 2019 was struck down in *Hindustan Construction Company Ltd. v. Union of India*. The 2021 amendment added a proviso requiring an unconditional stay where the court is prima facie satisfied that the agreement, the contract or the making of the award was induced by fraud or corruption. Second, **Section 9(3)** bars a court from entertaining an application for interim measures once the tribunal is constituted, unless the remedy under **Section 17** would not be efficacious — a provision that rewards a party which moves early and penalises one that waits.

A draft Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Bill was circulated for public consultation in 2024. Proposals under consultation are not law, and nothing in a contract should be drafted on the assumption that they will be enacted in any particular form.

### Mediation

The **Mediation Act, 2023** (Act 32 of 2023) received assent on 14 September 2023. It establishes a statutory framework for mediation: **Section 5** provides for pre-litigation mediation on a voluntary basis with mutual consent; **Section 6** read with the **First Schedule** identifies disputes not fit for mediation; **Section 18** sets a 120-day timetable extendable by 60 days; **Section 19** deals with the mediated settlement agreement; **Section 27** makes such an agreement enforceable as a judgment or decree; and **Section 28** permits a challenge only on the grounds of fraud, corruption, impersonation, or that the dispute was not fit for mediation, within 90 days extendable by a further 90 days.

**A point that is frequently stated incorrectly.** The Act's commencement was staged. Notification S.O. 4384(E) dated 9 October 2023 brought into force Section 1, Section 3, Section 26, Sections 31 to 38, Sections 45 to 47, Sections 50 to 54 and Sections 56 to 57 — that is, the definitions, the provisions establishing the Mediation Council of India, the Mediation Fund, the rule-making powers and the transitional provisions. The operative provisions on pre-litigation mediation, timelines, settlement agreements, enforcement and challenge were not in that notification. **Before relying on any provision of the Mediation Act, 2023 in a clause or in a pleading, the commencement position should be confirmed against the current gazette.** A contract that routes disputes to "mediation under the Mediation Act, 2023" without alternative machinery may route them nowhere.

What does govern commercial disputes now is **Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015**, inserted by the 2018 amendment. A suit of Specified Value — not less than ₹3,00,000 under **Section 2(1)(i)** of that Act — that does not contemplate urgent interim relief cannot be instituted unless the plaintiff has exhausted pre-institution mediation under the Pre-Institution Mediation and Settlement Rules, 2018. In *M/s Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd.*, the Supreme Court held this requirement mandatory, with non-compliance attracting rejection of the plaint under **Order VII Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908**. The urgent-interim-relief carve-out is the pressure point, and it is litigated: a plaint that claims urgency to bypass mediation invites a challenge on that very ground.

## Five drafting failures that surface only in a dispute

### One: the clause that does not say where

Naming a city as the "venue" without addressing the seat leaves the supervisory court to be determined by construction. Following *Arif Azim*, a named venue will usually be read as the seat, but "usually" is not a basis on which to plan. State the seat expressly, state the venue separately if hearings are to be held elsewhere, and state the governing law of the arbitration agreement as distinct from the governing law of the contract.

### Two: the clause that cannot be operated

Clauses referring to institutions that have been renamed, merged or never existed; clauses requiring an appointing authority that has no power to appoint; clauses prescribing three arbitrators for a contract worth less than the cost of three arbitrators. These are pathological clauses, and each one converts a reference into an application under **Section 11 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996** before the dispute has begun. The same discipline applies to every operative clause in a commercial contract, as discussed in our note on [the Indian Contract Act framework and enforceability](/insights/indian-contract-act-framework-enforceability-practitioner-guide/).

### Three: the multi-tier clause with no exit

Escalation clauses — negotiation, then senior management, then mediation, then arbitration — are commercially sensible and frequently drafted as mandatory conditions precedent without any time limit. The result is that a party wishing to arbitrate must first prove it has exhausted steps the counterparty can prolong indefinitely. Each tier needs a defined trigger, a defined duration and an automatic right to proceed on expiry.

### Four: silence on interim relief

Given **Section 9(3) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**, the window for court-ordered interim measures narrows once the tribunal is constituted. A clause that provides for emergency arbitration, or that expressly preserves the parties' right to approach a court for interim protection, is doing real work. A clause silent on the point leaves a party needing urgent protection to litigate the question of where it may ask.

### Five: ignoring the enforcement endpoint

The purpose of the clause is an enforceable award. For a cross-border contract that means checking whether the chosen seat is in a country notified by the Central Government under **Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**, and whether the relationship is commercial under Indian law. A foreign award from an unnotified seat is not enforceable in India under Part II however impeccable the arbitration. For a domestic contract it means considering, at the drafting stage, where the counterparty's assets are and whether execution under Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 will reach them.
